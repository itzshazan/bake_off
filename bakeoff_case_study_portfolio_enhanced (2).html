01 — The Beginning

The Challenge That
Hooked Me

Our Bakeoff Competition gave us a deceptively simple brief: predict California housing prices using census data. The dataset had been around for decades. Thousands of Kaggle notebooks existed. It felt almost too familiar.

But here's what made it genuinely difficult — and genuinely interesting: we weren't just asked to get a high accuracy score. We were asked to understand why the model works. To pick an algorithm, map it to a deep learning concept, and defend every single choice across 15 iterative versions.

That constraint changed everything. I was assigned Lasso Regression — and its connection to Dropout in neural networks. Suddenly this wasn't a standard regression problem. It was about teaching myself the geometry of sparsity.

"I had no idea that forcing coefficients to zero would one day make me understand why neural networks work the way they do."

— Reflective note, Week 1

The Problem Statement

Forecast residential property values across California using geographic coordinates, structural data, and socio-economic indicators. Output: median house value per census block in USD.

My Specific Algorithm

Lasso Regression (L1 Regularization) — with an explicit requirement to draw parallels to Dropout in deep neural networks. The goal was to show that sparsity is a universal principle across ML paradigms.

The Team

Five members, five algorithms. Eshaan (lead), Ashir, Shivam, Shazan, and Ashish — each responsible for one model type, working in parallel under Dr. Manpreet Kaur's supervision.

02 — The Starting Point

Honest About
How Lost I Was

  • Ran the model without scaling — low-magnitude features were being unfairly penalized by the L1 term

  • Left ocean_proximity as a string column — the encoder would crash with a type error mid-fit

03 — Research & Learning

Going Slow to
Go Fast Later

Before touching more code, I stepped back. I read Scikit-Learn's Lasso documentation properly. I watched 3Blue1Brown's intuition videos on linear algebra. I found a paper on L1 vs L2 regularization by Tibshirani. Then I went back to the data.

The biggest conceptual shift came when I understood why L1 creates sparsity while L2 doesn't — it's a geometric argument about how the penalty contour intersects the loss surface at corners rather than on faces. Once that clicked, the rest of the project made sense.

I also spent a day just profiling the dataset — correlation matrices, distribution plots, geographic scatter plots. That's when I spotted the multicollinearity problem: total_rooms, total_bedrooms, population, and households were all saying roughly the same thing in different units.

04 — Feature Engineering

Building the
Right Inputs

Raw census counts were noisy and size-biased. A neighborhood with 1,000 total rooms looks very different from one with 10 — but the per-household ratio tells the real story. Three engineered ratios transformed the feature space:

rooms_per_household

total_rooms / (households + 1) — average living space size per unit

bedrooms_per_room

total_bedrooms / (total_rooms + 1) — layout density and house type signal

population_per_household

population / (households + 1) — neighborhood occupancy density

All three of these engineered features survived Lasso's feature elimination at Alpha = 500 — validating that they carried genuine predictive signal, not noise. The model agreed with the intuition.

05 — Model Training

15 Versions,
One Champion

The hyperparameter sweep wasn't just about finding the best number — it was about understanding what each value of Alpha actually does to a model's behaviour. Here's how the story unfolded across four phases:

Phase 1

V1–V4

Low Regularization — Alpha 0.01 to 10

All 15 features active. The L1 penalty existed mathematically but was too small to trigger any feature elimination. Models behaved identically to standard multiple linear regression. Test R² hovered around 63.57%. I learned that Lasso only starts doing something interesting well above Alpha = 1.

No sparsity yet

Phase 2

V5–V7

Optimal Zone — Alpha 50 to 500

This is where things got genuinely interesting. At Alpha = 100 (V6), the first feature dropped to zero. At Alpha = 500 (V7), two features were eliminated entirely while test accuracy actually improved to 63.74% — higher than the all-features versions. The model was becoming smarter by forgetting irrelevant information.

Champion: V7, Alpha = 500

Phase 3

V8–V10

Aggressive Sparsity — Alpha 1,000 to 10,000

At Alpha = 10,000 only three features survived: median_income, latitude, and longitude. Test R² collapsed to 51.73%. This was the Dropout analogy made concrete — eliminating too many neurons/features destroys the model's capacity to capture signal. The graph of accuracy vs Alpha shows a clear inverted-U shape.

Underfitting begins

Phase 4

V11–V15

Strategy & Constraint Experiments

Switching to random coordinate descent (V11–V12) produced nearly identical results to cyclic, confirming convergence stability. The most revealing experiment: V14 forced all coefficients to be non-negative (Positive=True) and performance dropped to 56.51% — proving that some features like latitude have genuinely negative relationships with price. Artificial constraints destroy real signals.

V14 teaches by failing

07 — Challenges & Mistakes

The Failures That
Taught the Most

What tripped me up:

  • Forgot StandardScaler entirely in V1 — the L1 penalty hit latitude/longitude disproportionately since they were in degree units, not standardized
  • Didn't remove outliers before training — IQR-based removal wasn't applied, making predictions at extreme values wildly off
  • Kept multicollinear features naively — the four raw count columns were all present, creating redundancy that confused the solver
  • Interpreted V14's drop in R² as a bug in my code — spent hours debugging before realizing the issue was the Positive=True constraint forcing invalid sign assumptions

How each got fixed:

  • Added StandardScaler inside the pipeline — all features now at mean=0, std=1 before the L1 penalty sees them
  • Added IQR outlier removal step before train/test split — stabilized the residual distribution considerably
  • Trusted L1 to handle multicollinearity automatically — it picks the representative and zeroes the rest. No manual dropping needed
  • V14 became a deliberate experiment in the writeup — the failure was documented as proof that sign constraints destroy valid negative signals

08 — Breakthrough

The Moment It
Finally Clicked

The breakthrough wasn't a single moment — it was a shift in mindset. I stopped thinking about Alpha as a dial to tune for accuracy and started thinking about it as a dial that controls how much the model is allowed to remember.

When V7 (Alpha = 500) dropped two features and test accuracy went up, something clicked. Those two eliminated features weren't adding signal — they were adding noise. The model's decision to forget them was correct, and measurably so.

That's the Dropout analogy made real: moderate forgetting improves generalization. Aggressive forgetting destroys capacity. Finding the right balance is the whole game.

"Accuracy improved by discarding information. That felt counterintuitive until it felt inevitable."

09 — The Dropout Analogy

L1 Sparsity &
Deep Learning Connections

One of the required deliverables was drawing a rigorous parallel between Lasso's L1 penalty and Dropout regularization in neural networks. It turned out to be more than an analogy — it's the same underlying principle.

Feature zeroing vs neuron zeroing

Lasso forces weak feature weights exactly to zero. Dropout forces random neuron activations to zero. Both create sparse representations.

Deterministic vs stochastic

Lasso always selects the same features (deterministic). Dropout samples different neurons per batch (stochastic). Same goal, different mechanism.

Co-dependency prevention

L1 prevents feature co-dependency by selecting one representative from correlated groups. Dropout prevents neuron co-adaptation by forcing independent learning.

Generalization through sparsity

Both achieve better generalization not by adding more parameters — but by constraining which ones are allowed to contribute to predictions.

10 — Final Outcome

What We Actually
Built

The final pipeline was clean, defensible, and reproducible. Every step had a reason. Every version had a documented hypothesis and a measured outcome. The result: a Lasso model that generalized better to unseen data than it fit the training set — a clear signal that regularization did its job.

Final Pipeline Architecture

IQR Outlier Removal (20,640 → ~18,000 rows) → Median Imputation (total_bedrooms) → Feature Engineering (3 density ratios) → OneHotEncoding (ocean_proximity, drop=first) → StandardScaler (mean=0, std=1) → Lasso(alpha=500, max_iter=5000, tol=0.0001)

Key Result vs Benchmark

Champion (Lasso α=500): Test R² = 63.74%, RMSE = $57,908, MAE = $42,723. XGBoost benchmark: 83.75%. The 20-point gap quantifies the exact cost of the linearity constraint — and explains why gradient-boosted trees exist for tabular regression.

Automatic Feature Selection

At α=10,000, Lasso reduced to exactly 3 features: median_income, latitude, longitude — the irreducible geographic-economic core. No human curation. L1 regularization as a feature selection oracle, empirically proven across 15 controlled experiments.

11 — Personal Reflection

What I Learned
Beyond the Numbers

When I started this project, I thought "machine learning" meant finding the right algorithm and running it. By the end, I understood that it means making defensible choices, measuring honestly, and being willing to be wrong in version 11 if version 12 is better for it.

The 15-version iteration structure forced something most courses don't: patience. I couldn't skip to the champion model because I didn't know it was the champion yet. I had to discover it by being systematic — and that discipline transferred into how I approach every technical problem now.

I also learned something about honesty in engineering. V14 failed badly because I imposed an assumption that wasn't supported by the data. Writing that up openly, as a negative result with a clear explanation, felt more valuable than hiding it. Failure is evidence too.

The Dropout parallel was the most intellectually satisfying part. Seeing that a concept from 1996-era linear models and a concept from 2014-era deep learning both reduce to the same geometric principle — sparse representations prevent overfitting — made the whole discipline feel more unified and less like a bag of tricks.

This wasn't just a competition. It was the first time I felt like an engineer rather than a student running someone else's code.
